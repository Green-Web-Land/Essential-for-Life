# 3. Match communication to understanding

[Course home](../README.md) · [Curriculum](../CURRICULUM.md)

## Objective

Choose a manageable way to communicate with the particular child.

## Explanation

Age helps you choose a starting point, not a script everyone must meet. The following are editorial examples, not milestone tests.

| Context | Possible starting point |
|---|---|
| Infancy | Respond to sounds, movement and needs; do not demand an explanation. |
| Early childhood | One concrete step, a demonstration or a picture. |
| School years | Invite the child's explanation and work through a manageable next step. |
| Adolescence | Ask directly, allow privacy and discuss reasons without treating the young person as a small child. |

Use the person's usual language or communication method where possible. A child who understands complex ideas may still need extra response time or nonspoken expression. Don't require eye contact as proof of listening. Check the task itself before treating difficulty as unwillingness. For developmental concerns, see [lesson 10](10-differences.md).

UNICEF advises clear language appropriate to the child. [Source](https://www.unicef.org/eca/stories/how-communicate-effectively-your-young-child). Our table illustrates choices; it does not establish when a skill should appear.

## Worked example

Fictional: “Get organized” leaves Sami unsure where to start. The caregiver tries, “Put the book in this bag,” while pointing. After waiting, they ask whether a picture of the next step would help. For a teenager, they might instead ask, “What would help you get ready on time?” Neither wording guarantees success.

## Practice

Rewrite “Act responsibly and sort everything out” for a fictional child confused by a cluttered art table. Supply one safe first action, one alternative way to understand it, and a way to check whether the instruction made sense.

## Answers and checking

For example: “Put the paper in this tray.” Point to the tray or show one sheet. Ask, “Was that clear, or shall I show you?” Avoid adding five more instructions before the child can start. An adult should handle dangerous or sharp items, not hide them inside the instruction “everything.”

## Try independently

Adapt the same situation for a teenager who asks you not to take over. Ask what support they want and explain any actual safety limit.
