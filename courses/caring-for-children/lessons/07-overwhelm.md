# 7. Respond to overwhelm and repair

[Course home](../README.md) · [Curriculum](../CURRICULUM.md)

## Objective

Reduce your own escalation and take responsibility for a mistake.

## Explanation

A distressed response can have many causes, including discomfort, fatigue, frustration or circumstances you do not know. Do not label every episode manipulation or diagnose it from a description. Check immediate safety and possible urgent health needs before treating it as a conversation problem.

Use fewer demands while the child is struggling. Offer a manageable pause or quieter setting if suitable; maintain needed supervision. If you need a break, arrange safe care rather than leaving a dependent child unattended. An adult who fears hurting a child needs immediate help from a safe adult or urgent service.

Later, repair names your action without making the child responsible for it. “I shouted. That wasn't okay. I'll try a quieter voice” differs from “You made me shout.” The child need not comfort you, forgive immediately or hug. These are communication examples, not crisis treatment.

![Own the action; Make a change; Allow a response](../images/repair.png)

Text equivalent: Own the action: I shouted. I am sorry.; No blame attached. Make a change: I will pause before speaking.; Follow through. Allow a response: You need not answer now.; No required hug.

## Worked example

Fictional: Pat spills paint. The caregiver yells, then sees Pat freeze. After checking safety: “I shouted. I'm sorry. We'll sort out the spill. You don't need to answer me now.” Later the caregiver puts a protective mat ready, reducing the problem without making Pat manage the adult's feelings.

## Practice

Rewrite: “I'm sorry, but you always make me lose control. Give me a hug so we can forget it.” Include responsibility, a practical change, and freedom not to respond.

## Answers and checking

“I raised my voice. That was my action, and I'm sorry. Next time I'll pause before speaking. You don't have to reply now.” A promise needs follow-through. Removing “but you” stops shifting blame; removing the required hug preserves choice. Repeated harm needs outside support, not repeated apologies alone.

## Try independently

Fictional: two children are overwhelmed by a noisy gathering. Propose a supervised quieter option without assuming they have the same needs or want the same company.
