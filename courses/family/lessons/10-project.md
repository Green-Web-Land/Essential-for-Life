# 10. Final fictional family scenario

[Course home](../README.md) · [Glossary](../GLOSSARY.md)

**Goal:** Combine communication, consent and planning while recognising the limits of the exercise.

Adults 18+. All people and situations below are fictional. Work on paper; skip or replace any scenario you do not want to read. No personal disclosure or real conversation is required.

## Understand

Work only with the supplied fiction. This is an assessment of written reasoning, not an assessment of your family or your ability to repair a relationship. You may answer in notes, a table or a spoken response to a trusted facilitator. Do not submit private records.

Use the checklist in the facilitator guide. Several answers can meet the objective. A response must fit the facts, respect a refusal and identify missing information. A polished script that invents consent is not a strong answer.

## Worked example

Worked mini-example: Two cousins agree to share a shopping trip. One can drive; the other can prepare a list. A useful draft assigns those proposed contributions, asks who can pay and carry the bags, and seeks agreement on the time. “The driver will pay” is an unsupported addition. This illustrates the method without settling the larger exercise.

## Practise

Full scenario: Adult siblings Amal and Ben share a home with adult cousin Chris. A Saturday meal is proposed for 6 pm. Amal can prepare a menu Friday but cannot cook Saturday. Ben works until 5 and can wash dishes afterwards. Chris can cook from 4 to 5, but only if ingredients are ready. Nobody has agreed to shop. Chris says no photographs may be posted online. Ben says, “When plans arrive late, I cannot arrange my day.”

1. List three facts and two unknowns.
2. Write a listening check for Ben.
3. Make one concrete request about shopping, allowing a refusal.
4. Draft a task/time agreement with an unresolved item marked.
5. Respond to Chris's photo boundary.
6. Propose a review point and an alternative if shopping cannot be arranged.
7. Separate variation: a person threatens Chris for refusing a photograph. Explain why the ordinary planning exercise must not be used to solve that threat.

## Suggested reasoning

Possible answer: Known facts include Amal's menu availability, Ben's work timing and Chris's cooking window. Unknowns include who can shop, budget and food requirements. Listening check: “Do you need earlier notice to plan your day?” Request: “Would you be able to shop before 4, or should we consider another meal plan?” This must be directed as a proposal, not assigned to Ben because he is available later.

A draft lists Amal/menu/Friday; Chris/cooking/4–5 Saturday/ingredients required; Ben/dishes/after meal; shopping/person and time unconfirmed. Ask whether serving at 6 is workable and how food would be held appropriately; this course does not supply food-safety instructions. Review after the menu is proposed Friday. If the necessary arrangements cannot be made, ask about changing the meal or date rather than promising it will happen.

Accept Chris's refusal and do not post their photograph. In variation 7, stop treating the issue as a group compromise; identify the threat and use lesson 9's appropriate help route. Do not require Chris to negotiate permission under pressure.

## Try a new situation

New setting: Two adult relatives plan a shared garden visit. One needs a step-free route; the other has only one hour. Write a plan with a missing-information question and an alternative if the route is unsuitable. Check: do not promise accessibility without checking the actual place.
