# 7. Apologies, repair and trust

[Course home](../README.md) · [Glossary](../GLOSSARY.md)

**Goal:** Distinguish a proposed repair from a demand for forgiveness.

Adults 18+. All people and situations below are fictional. Work on paper; skip or replace any scenario you do not want to read. No personal disclosure or real conversation is required.

## Understand

An apology can identify a specific action, acknowledge its effect and propose what the speaker will do differently. It cannot decide when another person should trust again. The exercise asks you to assess words and actions, not measure sincerity from tone or appearance.

Avoid “I am sorry you are so sensitive,” which shifts attention to the other person's reaction. A clearer statement owns the action. Repair should address the practical harm where possible and respect the affected person's wishes. An explanation can provide context without cancelling responsibility.

A promise is evidence of an intention, not evidence that the change has happened. Trust may depend on later conduct; renewed contact, forgiveness and reconciliation are not requirements of this course.

## Worked example

Eli returned a borrowed tool late after promising Tuesday. Eli says: “I returned it Friday, so you could not use it Wednesday. I am sorry. I will return borrowed items by the agreed time or ask in advance before changing the arrangement. Is there a practical cost I should discuss with you?” The lender can decline future loans. Eli should not insist that the apology earns another loan.

## Practise

Rae shared a private invitation without permission. Compare “Sorry, but everyone shares things” with an apology that names the action and one realistic repair. Do not promise that every forwarded copy can be recovered.

## Suggested reasoning

One answer: “I shared your invitation without asking. I am sorry. I can remove my own post and ask recipients not to forward it, but I cannot promise all copies disappear. I will ask before sharing in future.” It owns the action and names limits. It does not require the other person to accept the apology or disclose more.

## Try a new situation

A relative says they are not ready to discuss an apology. Write a response that allows space. Check that it does not set a deadline for forgiveness.
