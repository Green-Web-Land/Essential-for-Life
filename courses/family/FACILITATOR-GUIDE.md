# Facilitator guide

[Course home](README.md)

Use this adult introductory course as educational practice, not group therapy or screening. Read the material first and be clear about your actual role and the limits of support you can provide.

## Set up an accessible session

Provide the fictional text in advance where possible. Accept written notes, labelled diagrams or spoken answers. Do not grade eye contact, accent, emotional expression or willingness to share. Offer individual work rather than compulsory paired role-play. Learners may pass without explaining why.

Keep activities about the supplied characters. Do not ask “Has this happened to you?” or require a family member to assess the learner. Avoid collecting identifiable stories in shared documents or public issues.

If someone raises a real safety concern, stop treating their situation as an exercise. Do not investigate in front of the group, promise absolute confidentiality or offer an improvised treatment or safety plan. Use your setting's applicable safeguarding procedures and appropriate qualified support; establish those responsibilities before facilitating. See lesson 9 for Canadian reference routes, not a complete protocol for your organization.

## Neutral alternatives

For lessons 6 and 9, a learner may instead compare a fictional library room-booking question, an access-support enquiry and a building emergency. Supply the relevant local emergency instructions yourself if teaching in a real venue; do not invent them. Assess the ability to distinguish ordinary coordination from a need for specialist or emergency assistance. The alternative does not demonstrate learning about family violence specifically.

For lesson 7, use a late return of a board game instead of a privacy breach. For lesson 10, use planning a voluntary community display with three fictional adults and a refusal to publish a photograph. Do not require the threat variation; an alternative can ask which support category fits a supplied emergency notice.

## Final-project rubric

Score each row 0 (missing or contradicts the facts), 1 (partly supported), or 2 (clear and supported). Maximum 14. The total is feedback for this exercise, not a safety score or qualification. Revise any answer that pressures consent or treats threats as shared blame regardless of its total.

| Criterion | Full-credit evidence for lesson 10 |
|---|---|
| Facts and unknowns | At least three supplied facts and two genuine unknowns; no invented motives. |
| Listening | Checks Ben's meaning and permits correction. |
| Request | Specific shopping proposal with a real opportunity to refuse or suggest an alternative. |
| Agreement | Records the three stated contributions and leaves shopping unassigned; recognises prerequisites. |
| Consent | Accepts Chris's refusal and does not post the photograph. |
| Review | Proposes a check before cooking depends on missing ingredients and a realistic alternative. |
| Limits and help | Distinguishes the threat variation from an ordinary planning disagreement and identifies suitable help without confrontation instructions. |

## Worked assessment examples

“Ben finishes at 5, so he will shop at 3” contradicts the supplied work timing: agreement criterion 0. “Shopping is not assigned; ask who can obtain ingredients before Chris starts” identifies the gap: 2 if the remaining agreement is also complete.

“We can post because everyone else agrees” earns 0 for consent. “Do not post Chris's photograph; approval from others does not replace Chris's refusal” earns 2.

“Chris should apologise to calm the threatening person” does not meet the limits/help criterion. Recognising the threat and choosing an appropriate support route meets it without diagnosing anyone.

After feedback, ask the learner to explain one revision and try the garden-visit transfer task. Judge only the written reasoning. Do not claim a learner pilot or outcome validation has occurred.
