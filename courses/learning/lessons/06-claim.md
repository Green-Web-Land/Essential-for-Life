# 6. Read a claim carefully

[Course home](../README.md) · [Glossary](../GLOSSARY.md)

**Goal:** Identify the scope and evidence a sentence needs.

Adults 18+. Supplied practice records are fictional. Use paper, speech or an accessible equivalent; calculator optional.

## Understand

Before agreeing or disagreeing, identify what the claim says: about whom, doing what, where, when and compared with what. Words such as “all,” “best” and “always” can extend beyond the supplied evidence.

Separate a result from its interpretation. A count of completed tasks is not automatically a measure of long-term learning, and an immediate result does not establish a future outcome.

A source's date matters in context: an old record may describe its own period accurately while being insufficient for a present-day claim. A recent publication may still repeat old evidence.

## Worked example

Fictional notice: “On 12 May, 8 of 10 volunteers completed one sorting task immediately after a demonstration.” This concerns one task and ten volunteers. “Everyone will remember every rule forever” is a different, unsupported claim.

## Practise

List three missing facts before treating the notice as evidence that a teaching method caused better long-term learning. Then rewrite it without expanding the supplied result.

## Suggested reasoning

Missing facts could include prior knowledge, comparison conditions, participant selection and a later retention check. Rewrite: “The notice reports 8 completions among 10 volunteers on one immediate task.” It does not show a causal or long-term effect.

## Try a new situation

Another notice says “twice as many succeeded” without group sizes. What should you request in principle? Check: counts, denominators, outcome definition and comparison period. No actual contact is required.
