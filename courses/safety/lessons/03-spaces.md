# 3. Everyday spaces and equipment

[Course home](../README.md) · [Curriculum](../CURRICULUM.md)

## Goal

Recognise a concern without attempting a hazardous fix.

## Understand

A shared space has people responsible for its upkeep. Reporting a specific problem can be useful even when you cannot identify its cause. Report the location and what you observed; do not label something repaired or safe merely because a message was sent.

Damaged electrical equipment needs appropriate attention, not an improvised repair by a beginner. [Source S8](../SOURCES.md) A blocked designated exit also requires attention from the responsible building staff. Do not use a paper exercise to decide which real alternative route is safe. Building-specific emergency arrangements matter.

## Worked example

Fictional case E1: A drawing card says, “Two heavy cabinets obstruct the marked exit; building reception is open; no emergency is reported.” Sam can tell reception the exact location. The card does not authorise Sam to shift heavy furniture alone. Case E2 is the unplugged split cord from Lesson 1; wrapping it and trying it is not a course activity.

## Paper activity

For E1 write a two-sentence report and one follow-up question. For E2 choose: keep unused and seek appropriate help; open the plug; or test it quickly. Explain why a report alone is not proof the concern is resolved.

## Suggested reasoning

Possible report: “The marked exit beside fictional Room B is obstructed by two cabinets. Please arrange appropriate attention.” Follow-up: “Who is handling this and what should visitors do meanwhile?” E2: keep unused and seek appropriate help. A sent report establishes communication, not repair. If an emergency develops, use emergency arrangements rather than waiting for a routine reply.

## Try the distinction again

Write a question a beginner could ask before using unfamiliar equipment: who can provide the correct instructions or training? No practical inspection or repair is required.
