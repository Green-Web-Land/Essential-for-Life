# 4. Meet the solar system

[Course home](../README.md) · [Glossary](../GLOSSARY.md)

**Goal:** Classify familiar objects using more than their appearance.

Adults 18+. All invented records and model numbers are labelled. Work on paper or an accessible equivalent; calculator optional.

## Understand

Our solar system includes the Sun, planets, dwarf planets, moons and smaller bodies. The eight planets, outward from the Sun, are Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune. This list gives order, not equal spacing.

The Sun is a star. A moon is a natural satellite; it need not be the same size or shape as our Moon. In the International Astronomical Union's solar-system definition summarized by NASA, being a planet involves orbiting the Sun, being nearly round through self-gravity, and gravitational dominance in its orbital neighbourhood. A dwarf planet does not meet that last criterion. “Cleared” does not mean an orbit contains absolutely nothing else.

These criteria are not a universal shortcut for classifying every object from a photograph. Asteroids and comets add diversity; no current discovery count is needed for this lesson.

## Worked example

A fictional card says “a round object.” That is insufficient to choose planet, dwarf planet or moon. Information about its orbit and context is missing.

## Practise

Match each real name to the stated category: Sun, Earth, Earth's Moon, Pluto. Categories: star, planet, natural satellite, dwarf planet. Then explain why a large-looking circle in a picture is not enough to assign a category.

## Suggested reasoning

Sun: star; Earth: planet; Earth's Moon: natural satellite; Pluto: dwarf planet. An illustration may enlarge objects, and appearance alone does not supply orbital context or the full classification criteria.

## Try a new situation

A diagram places planets equally far apart for readability. Write its missing note. Check: “Order shown; spacing and object sizes are not to scale.”
